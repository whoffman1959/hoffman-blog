**DAY 75 – OVERCOME WITH FEAR**

**Scripture**

**Esther 4:1-8 CSB**

When Mordecai learned all that had occurred, he tore his clothes, put on sackcloth and ashes, went into the middle of the city, and cried loudly and bitterly. [2] He went only as far as the King's Gate, since the law prohibited anyone wearing sackcloth from entering the King's Gate. [3] There was great mourning among the Jewish people in every province where the king's command and edict reached. They fasted, wept, and lamented, and many lay in sackcloth and ashes. [4] Esther's female servants and her eunuchs came and reported the news to her, and the queen was overcome with fear. She sent clothes for Mordecai to wear so that he would take off his sackcloth, but he did not accept them. [5] Esther summoned Hathach, one of the king's eunuchs who attended her, and dispatched him to Mordecai to learn what he was doing and why. [6] So Hathach went out to Mordecai in the city square in front of the King's Gate. [7] Mordecai told him everything that had happened as well as the exact amount of money Haman had promised to pay the royal treasury for the slaughter of the Jews. [8] Mordecai also gave him a copy of the written decree issued in Susa ordering their destruction, so that Hathach might show it to Esther, explain it to her, and command her to approach the king, implore his favor, and plead with him personally for her people.

**Sackcloth and Ashes**

There is always sadness and fear where evil is allowed to flourish. Mordecai had learned of Haman's terrible decree against the Jewish people, and he was overcome with grief. He tore his clothes, put on sackcloth and ashes, and went into the middle of the city, crying loudly and bitterly. He made his way toward the King's Gate but could go no farther, for those wearing sackcloth were not permitted to enter.

Mordecai must have understood that Haman's hatred had begun with his refusal to bow before him. What had started as a conflict between two men had now grown into a threat against an entire people. We are not told everything Mordecai was thinking, but we can imagine the weight of knowing that his people faced destruction because of one man's pride and hatred.

We sometimes see how jealousy, resentment, and dishonesty can bring suffering into the lives of others. In workplaces, communities, and even among friends, a person's reputation can be damaged by rumors or accusations that are not true. Someone who is simply trying to do what is right may find themselves in a situation where their words are misunderstood, their actions questioned, and their ability to defend themselves limited.

These situations can become especially painful when others accept what they hear without seeking the truth. Relationships may be damaged, opportunities lost, and innocent people left carrying burdens they never expected. Sometimes the truth eventually becomes known, while at other times the harm cannot be easily undone.

We can also see the damage caused by dishonesty in personal relationships and families. Trust can be weakened by interference, jealousy, or words spoken with harmful intentions. The consequences can reach far beyond the people directly involved, leaving others confused, wounded, and uncertain about what to do next.

There are times when we may feel powerless to change what is happening around us. It is a feeling most of us have experienced at some point in our lives. We may face circumstances beyond our control, problems we cannot solve, or situations in which every effort to make things right seems to fail. Yet it is often during these moments that we begin to understand how much we need God.

Before we reach the limits of our own strength, we can turn to our heavenly Father in prayer. We may not always receive the answer we expect, and our circumstances may not change immediately, but we can find comfort in knowing that God hears us and has not abandoned us. Many people have discovered that the very troubles that brought them to their knees also brought them into a closer relationship with Him.

In biblical times, wearing sackcloth and ashes was an outward expression of deep grief, mourning, humiliation, or repentance. It was a visible sign of distress, often accompanied by fasting and prayer. Throughout the Persian provinces, the Jewish people responded to Haman's decree with great mourning. They fasted, wept, and lamented, and many lay in sackcloth and ashes.

Imagine the fear that must have spread among families as they learned that a royal decree had been issued calling for their destruction. They had homes, children, occupations, and lives they had built throughout the kingdom. Now their future was threatened by an order bearing the authority of the king himself.

News of Mordecai's mourning eventually reached the inner palace. Esther's female servants and eunuchs reported what was happening, and the queen was overcome with fear. She sent clothing to Mordecai, hoping he would remove his sackcloth, but he refused.

We are not told exactly what Esther understood at this point. Perhaps she feared for Mordecai, perhaps she was troubled by the reports of mourning throughout the city, or perhaps she sensed that something terrible had happened. Whatever her concerns, she knew she needed to learn more.

Esther summoned Hathach, one of the king's eunuchs assigned to attend her, and sent him to Mordecai to discover what was happening and why.

Mordecai explained everything to Hathach, including the amount of silver Haman had promised to pay into the royal treasury in exchange for the destruction of the Jewish people. He also gave Hathach a copy of the written decree issued in Susa, instructing him to show it to Esther and explain what it meant.

Then Mordecai made a request that would change the course of Esther's life.

He wanted her to approach the king, seek his favor, and plead for the lives of her people.

Esther had become queen of Persia, but even her position did not mean she could approach the king whenever she wished. As we will discover in the next passage, appearing before the king without being summoned could place her own life in danger.

The stage was now set for a difficult decision. Esther could remain silent and hope to escape the danger, or she could risk her position and possibly her life by speaking on behalf of her people.

Sometimes doing what is right requires courage, especially when we cannot be certain of the outcome.

This brings us to another moment of fear recorded in Matthew chapter 8.

Jesus had been teaching the people, healing the sick, and demonstrating the power and compassion of God. After a busy time of ministry, He entered a boat with His disciples to cross the Sea of Galilee.

As they traveled, a violent storm arose. The waves began to sweep over the boat, threatening to swamp it. Some of the disciples were experienced fishermen who understood the dangers of the sea, yet even they were frightened by the strength of the storm.

And where was Jesus?

He was asleep.

Here we see something remarkable about our Savior. Jesus, who possessed authority over creation itself, had taken on human flesh and experienced the weariness that comes with being human. While the storm raged around them, He rested in the boat.

The disciples, fearing for their lives, woke Him and cried out for help.

Jesus asked why they were afraid and called attention to their little faith. Then He rose and rebuked the winds and the sea, and a great calm followed.

The disciples were amazed. Even the winds and the sea obeyed Him.

Think about that moment. These men had reached the limits of their own abilities. No amount of experience or physical strength could guarantee their safety. Yet they were not alone in the boat.

Jesus was with them.

We may not face a storm upon the Sea of Galilee, but we will face storms in our own lives. Some come through illness, financial hardship, or the loss of someone we love. Others come through broken relationships, injustice, or circumstances we never expected and cannot control.

When these storms come, do we immediately depend upon our own strength, trying to solve every problem ourselves? Or do we turn to the One who has authority over the winds and the sea?

The disciples did not demonstrate perfect faith that day, but they knew enough to turn to Jesus when they were afraid.

And that is where we should turn as well.

God does not promise that every storm will disappear the moment we pray. Sometimes He brings deliverance by changing our circumstances, and sometimes He gives us the strength and courage to endure what lies ahead. Either way, we can trust that He is with us.

Mordecai and the Jewish people were facing a storm that seemed impossible to overcome. Esther was about to be asked to step into that storm herself, uncertain of what the king might do.

Yet the God who watches over His people had not abandoned them.

As we continue through Esther, we will see how courage, faith, and the providence of God can bring hope even when circumstances appear hopeless.

When we feel powerless, we are not without hope. We have a heavenly Father who hears our prayers, knows our troubles, and invites us to place our trust in Him.

The storms may be greater than our strength, but they are never greater than our God.

**Reflection Questions**

1. Is there a situation in your life today that seems beyond your control? Have you brought it before Jesus in prayer and trusted Him with the outcome?

2. Do you know someone who is suffering because of dishonesty, unfair treatment, or difficult circumstances? Is there something you can do to offer encouragement, support, or help?

3. How have times of trouble or fear brought you closer to God? When the storms of life come, do you turn to Him first, or do you try to face them in your own strength?
