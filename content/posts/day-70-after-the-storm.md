**DAY 70 – CONSEQUENCES**

**Scripture**

**Esther 2:1-7 CSB**

Some time later, when King Ahasuerus's rage had cooled down, he remembered Vashti, what she had done, and what was decided against her. [2] The king's personal attendants suggested, "Let a search be made for beautiful young virgins for the king. [3] Let the king appoint commissioners in each province of his kingdom, so that they may gather all the beautiful young virgins to the harem at the fortress of Susa. Put them under the supervision of Hegai, the king's eunuch, keeper of the women, and give them the required beauty treatments. [4] Then the young woman who pleases the king will become queen instead of Vashti." This suggestion pleased the king, and he did accordingly. [5] In the fortress of Susa, there was a Jewish man named Mordecai son of Jair, son of Shimei, son of Kish, a Benjaminite. [6] Kish had been taken into exile from Jerusalem with the other captives when King Nebuchadnezzar of Babylon took King Jeconiah of Judah into exile. [7] Mordecai was the legal guardian of his cousin Hadassah (that is, Esther), because she had no father or mother. The young woman had a beautiful figure and was extremely good-looking. When her father and mother died, Mordecai had adopted her as his own daughter.

**Fruit of Anger**

In James chapter 1, we read:

My dear brothers and sisters, understand this: Everyone should be quick to listen, slow to speak, and slow to anger, for human anger does not accomplish God's righteousness.

When I look back over my life, I can remember times when anger took hold of me, and in that anger, I spoke words that should never have been said. Once those words leave our mouths, they cannot be taken back. We can apologize and try to make amends, but the sting remains. With time, the pain may become less noticeable, yet the memory of those words can linger.

Someone once said that the pen is mightier than the sword, and history has shown us the power of words. Words can inspire people to do great things, but they can also stir anger, division, and even violence. Sometimes words are what set the sword in motion.

I remember a time when I was teaching. I was struggling with health problems that we later discovered were caused by cancer. Financial difficulties were piling up, and the demands and expectations of my work seemed overwhelming. In my heart, I wanted to be a good teacher, and I loved my students.

But one day, as I stood outside my classroom, the students were making so much noise that they could be heard down the hallway. Other teachers looked at me as though I should do something, as though I was failing to maintain control of my classroom.

My pride was wounded, my body was weak, and I felt pushed beyond my limits. In one moment of anger, I yelled at my students. The moment the words left my mouth, I wished I could take them back.

The principal heard my outburst, and the consequences affected the remainder of my teaching career. My health continued to deteriorate, and what should have been the closing chapter of a career spent helping young people became stained by that single moment.

How often have we lost control of our tongues? I have certainly had moments that I have needed to lay at the feet of Jesus, asking for forgiveness and the strength not to repeat them. Anger does not accomplish the righteousness of God, and when we allow it to control our words and actions, it can damage our witness for Christ.

When King Ahasuerus's rage finally subsided, he remembered Queen Vashti, her refusal, and the decree that had been issued against her. The festivities were over, and the excitement and anger of that moment had passed.

Yet the decision remained. Under the decree he had approved, Vashti was no longer permitted to enter his presence, and her royal position was to be given to another woman. Scripture does not tell us whether the king regretted what had happened, but he could not simply return to the way things had been.

His personal attendants proposed a search throughout the kingdom for beautiful young virgins who would be brought to the fortress at Susa. There, under the supervision of Hegai, the keeper of the women, they would receive the required beauty treatments. One of these young women would eventually be chosen to replace Vashti as queen.

The king approved their suggestion, and preparations began.

God can work even through our mistakes and the consequences of our anger. What began with the king's outburst and the removal of Queen Vashti would eventually become part of a much greater story.

In the fortress of Susa lived a Jewish man named Mordecai. He was the guardian of his young cousin Hadassah, whom we know as Esther. Her parents had died, and Mordecai had adopted her as his own daughter. Scripture tells us that Esther was a beautiful young woman.

Neither Mordecai nor Esther could have known what lay ahead. Yet through the events unfolding in the Persian kingdom, God was preparing the way for Esther to play a part in protecting His people.

We may not be able to undo the words we have spoken or the decisions we have made in anger. But through God's grace, we can seek forgiveness, learn from our mistakes, and ask Him to guide our actions in the days ahead.

The storm of anger may pass, but its consequences can remain. May we learn to be slow to anger, careful with our words, and willing to seek God's wisdom before we act.

**Reflection Questions**

1. Has there ever been a time when you said something or reacted in anger that you wish you could take back today?

2. Are there times when it is right for us to be angry? How can we keep that anger from leading us into sin?

3. The Bible tells us not to let the sun go down on our anger. What does this mean for how we should handle conflict and seek reconciliation?
