**DAY 80 – JUSTICE IS DONE**

**Scripture**

**Esther 7:1-10 CSB**

The king and Haman came to feast with Esther the queen. [2] Once again, on the second day while drinking wine, the king asked Esther, "Queen Esther, whatever you ask will be given to you. Whatever you seek, even to half the kingdom, will be done." [3] Queen Esther answered, "If I have found favor with you, Your Majesty, and if the king is pleased, spare my life; this is my request. And spare my people; this is my desire. [4] For my people and I have been sold to destruction, death, and annihilation. If we had merely been sold as male and female slaves, I would have kept silent. Indeed, the trouble wouldn't be worth burdening the king." [5] King Ahasuerus spoke up and asked Queen Esther, "Who is this, and where is the one who would devise such a scheme?" [6] Esther answered, "The adversary and enemy is this evil Haman." Haman stood terrified before the king and queen. [7] The king arose in anger and went from where they were drinking wine to the palace garden. Haman remained to beg Queen Esther for his life because he realized the king was planning something terrible for him. [8] Just as the king returned from the palace garden to the banquet hall, Haman was falling on the couch where Esther was reclining. The king exclaimed, "Would he actually violate the queen while I am in the house?" As soon as the statement left the king's mouth, they covered Haman's face. [9] Harbona, one of the king's eunuchs, said, "There is a gallows seventy-five feet tall at Haman's house that he made for Mordecai, who gave the report that saved the king." The king said, "Hang him on it." [10] They hanged Haman on the gallows he had prepared for Mordecai. Then the king's anger subsided.

**The Gallows Used**

A fitting passage for what happened to Haman can be found in Psalm 7:15–16, where we read of a man who digs a pit to trap another, only to fall into the very hole he has made. The trouble he planned for someone else returns upon his own head. We find a similar warning in Galatians 6:7–8, where we are reminded that God cannot be mocked, for whatever a person sows, that person will also reap. As we return to the story of Esther, we are about to see these words illustrated in the life of Haman, a man whose pride and desire for revenge would bring about his own destruction.

The warning Haman had received from his wife and advisers was about to come true. He had arrived at the second banquet prepared by Queen Esther, but this time the evening would be very different from the one before. As the king, queen, and Haman enjoyed the banquet, King Ahasuerus once again asked Esther what she desired, promising to grant her request, even up to half his kingdom. Esther had waited for this moment, and now, in the presence of the very man who had plotted the destruction of her people, she made her appeal.

Rather than asking for wealth, power, or some special favor, Esther pleaded for her life and the lives of her people. She explained that they had been sold to destruction, death, and annihilation. Imagine the king's reaction as he heard his queen pleading for her very existence. Someone within his kingdom had arranged for her death, and he demanded to know who would dare devise such a scheme. Esther did not hesitate to identify the man responsible. The enemy was Haman, the very man who had been honored with an invitation to dine privately with the king and queen. In a moment, the favor Haman had enjoyed turned to terror as he stood before the king and queen, knowing that his carefully laid plans had been exposed.

Imagine Haman's fear at that moment. He had entered the palace expecting another evening of royal favor, but now the queen had exposed his plot before the king. The honor and influence he had enjoyed could no longer protect him.
King Ahasuerus rose in anger and went into the palace garden.

When anger overwhelms us, stepping away from a situation can sometimes be the wisest thing to do. It gives us time to settle our emotions, clear our thoughts, and seek God's guidance before taking action. We are not told why the king entered the garden, but his departure reminds us how important it is to avoid making decisions while controlled by anger. As Christians, we should seek the guidance of the Holy Spirit and approach others with a forgiving heart.

What a reversal of circumstances for Haman! Only a short time earlier, he had been boasting to his wife and friends about his wealth, his position, and the special honor of dining with the king and queen. He had expected another evening of royal favor, but now everything he valued was slipping away. The man who had planned the destruction of an entire people was suddenly pleading for his own life before a woman he had unknowingly condemned to death.

We can imagine the desperation in Haman's voice as he begged Esther for mercy. The king had left the room in anger, and Haman knew that his future rested upon what happened next. In his distress, he fell upon the couch where Esther was reclining. At that very moment, the king returned from the garden. What he saw only increased his anger, and he accused Haman of attempting to violate the queen in his own palace. Before Haman could recover from this new accusation, his face was covered, a sign that his fate had been decided.

Then Harbona, one of the king's eunuchs, brought another matter to the king's attention. Haman had constructed a gallows seventy-five feet tall for Mordecai, the very man who had saved the king's life by exposing an assassination plot. Imagine the bitter irony. Haman had built that towering structure expecting to rid himself of the one man who refused to honor him. Instead, his own pride and hatred had brought him before the king as a condemned man. The king ordered Haman to be executed upon the gallows he had prepared for Mordecai.

What a terrible ending for a man who had possessed so much wealth, authority, and influence! The very gallows Haman had prepared for Mordecai became the instrument of his own execution. His pride, jealousy, and desire for revenge had brought about his destruction.

But the story of Esther does not end with Haman's death.

In chapters 8 through 10, we learn that King Ahasuerus gave Haman's estate to Queen Esther. Mordecai was brought into the king's presence, received the signet ring formerly held by Haman, and was placed in charge of Haman's estate.
Yet the danger to the Jewish people remained. The decree Haman had arranged could not simply be revoked under Persian law. Esther again pleaded with the king, asking that her people be spared.

A new decree was issued, allowing the Jews throughout the empire to assemble and defend themselves against those who intended to destroy them. The message was sent throughout the provinces in their various languages.

What had been a time of fear and mourning became a time of hope and rejoicing. Mordecai left the king's presence dressed in royal garments of blue and white, wearing a great gold crown and a purple robe of fine linen. The city of Susa celebrated, and joy spread among the Jewish people throughout the empire.

When the appointed day arrived, the Jews defended themselves against their enemies. The provincial officials also supported them because they feared Mordecai's growing influence. Many enemies were killed, including Haman's ten sons, but Scripture specifically tells us that the Jews did not take the plunder they were permitted to seize.

In Susa, Esther requested another day for the Jews to act against their enemies, and the bodies of Haman's sons were displayed publicly. These events remind us that the deliverance described in Esther occurred during a violent and dangerous period in history. We should not overlook the seriousness of what happened, even as we recognize the survival of the Jewish people.

Afterward, the Jews celebrated their deliverance with feasting and gladness, sending gifts to one another and to the poor. These events became the foundation for the Jewish celebration of Purim.

Mordecai rose to become second only to King Ahasuerus. He used his position to seek the prosperity of his people and to speak for the well-being of their descendants.

What an extraordinary change from the beginning of the story! Esther, an orphan raised by her cousin Mordecai, became queen. Mordecai, who once sat at the King's Gate, became one of the most powerful men in the empire. And Haman, who sought to destroy them both, met his end upon the gallows he had constructed for another.

Throughout this account, we see how the plans of one man were overturned and how the Jewish people were preserved.

There is also an important lesson for us as followers of Jesus Christ.

When we are wronged or hurt by others, our natural desire may be to seek revenge. Yet Jesus teaches us a different way. He tells us to turn the other cheek and, when compelled to go one mile, to go two. We are called to respond with humility and love rather than allowing bitterness to control our hearts.

This does not mean we must approve of wrongdoing or allow others to continue harming us. It means that we can place our desire for revenge into God's hands and seek to live according to His will.

We serve a God who sees. He knows when we have been treated unfairly, and He knows the intentions of every heart. We do not need to take His place as judge.

As we close our study of Esther, perhaps the greatest lesson is that we can trust God even when we cannot see how He is working. Esther and Mordecai faced circumstances that seemed impossible, yet the events of their lives came together in ways neither could have anticipated.

Our calling is to remain faithful, to act with courage, and to glorify the Lord through the way we treat others.
